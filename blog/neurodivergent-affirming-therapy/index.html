What Is Neurodivergent-Affirming Therapy?

By Clay Klaus-Wade · October 16, 2026

The short answer

Neurodivergent-affirming therapy starts from a simple premise: autistic, ADHD, and otherwise neurodivergent minds aren't broken versions of "normal" ones. They're different ways of being human, with their own strengths, needs, and ways of making sense of the world. Affirming therapy doesn't try to make you seem less neurodivergent. It tries to help you live well as yourself.

That might sound obvious, but it's a real shift. For a long time, a lot of therapy for neurodivergent people focused on reducing visible differences: making eye contact, sitting still, hiding stims, fitting in. Many adults who went through that kind of support learned, more than anything, to hide.

Neurodivergence as identity

In affirming therapy, neurodivergence is understood as part of who you are, the way culture or sexuality is, rather than as a list of deficits to correct. That doesn't mean pretending there are no challenges. Executive function, sensory overload, and social exhaustion can be genuinely hard. It means locating the problem accurately: often, the struggle lives in the mismatch between how you're built and what the world around you expects, not in you.

What is masking, and why does it matter?

Masking, sometimes called camouflaging, is the effort many neurodivergent people put into appearing neurotypical: rehearsing conversations, copying other people's expressions, suppressing movement, or scripting small talk. In a 2017 study of 92 autistic adults, researchers found that people mostly masked to fit in and connect with others, and that it often came at a real cost, including exhaustion and a shaky sense of who they really were (Hull et al., 2017).

If you've ever come home from a perfectly pleasant day and needed hours to recover, or felt like you've performed a version of yourself for so long that you're not sure what's underneath, you may know exactly what this feels like.

What is autistic burnout?

Autistic burnout is a term that came from autistic people themselves long before researchers studied it. A 2020 study led by Dora Raymaker defined it as a state of deep exhaustion, loss of skills, and reduced tolerance for sensory input that reaches into every part of life, not just work (Raymaker et al., 2020). Participants described causes like the sheer, accumulated load of daily life, expectations that outpaced their capacity, and, most often, not having any way to rest and recharge.

Burnout isn't laziness, and it isn't a lack of willpower. Affirming therapy takes it seriously as a signal that something about the load you're carrying needs to change.

The double empathy problem

One idea I find especially important is what the autistic researcher Damian Milton called the double empathy problem. It suggests that misunderstandings between autistic and non-autistic people run in both directions; it isn't that autistic people lack empathy, but that people with very different ways of experiencing the world often struggle to read each other (National Autistic Society). That reframe can be a profound relief for people who have spent their lives being told the misunderstanding was entirely their fault.

What affirming therapy looks like in practice

Neurodivergent-affirming therapy is shaped around you. It might include:

  • Flexibility about how sessions work, like cameras off, fidgets welcome, breaks when you need them, or written follow-ups
  • Direct, clear communication without hidden expectations or social guessing games
  • Space to process a late diagnosis, including the grief, relief, and reinterpretation of your past that often come with it
  • Working on burnout recovery, unmasking at a pace that feels safe, and building a life that fits your actual needs
  • Support for anxiety, depression, relationships, or trauma, with a therapist who understands how neurodivergence shapes all of it

Frequently asked questions

What is neurodivergent-affirming therapy?

Neurodivergent-affirming therapy treats autism, ADHD, and other forms of neurodivergence as natural variations in how people think and experience the world. It focuses on helping you live well as yourself, not on making you appear neurotypical.

What is masking?

Masking, or camouflaging, is the effort to appear neurotypical, such as rehearsing conversations, copying expressions, or suppressing natural movements. Research shows it is often exhausting and can affect a person's sense of identity.

What is autistic burnout?

Autistic burnout is a state of deep exhaustion, loss of skills, and reduced sensory tolerance that affects every area of life. It is often caused by long-term stress and the lack of opportunities to rest and recover.

Can therapy help after a late autism or ADHD diagnosis?

Yes. Therapy can help you make sense of a late diagnosis, including the grief, relief, and new understanding of your past that often come with it, and build a life that fits your needs.
