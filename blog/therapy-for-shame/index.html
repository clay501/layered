Guilt vs. Shame: How Therapy Helps With Shame

By Clay Klaus-Wade · October 16, 2026

The short answer

Guilt says, "I did something bad." Shame says, "I am bad." That small shift in wording makes an enormous difference in how we feel and what we do next, and it's one of the reasons shame is so painful, so persistent, and so often at the heart of what brings people to therapy.

Therapy helps with shame not by arguing you out of it, but by giving it somewhere to go. Shame grows in hiding. It tends to loosen when it's spoken, met with understanding, and gently questioned by someone who sees more of you than the shame does.

What's the difference between guilt and shame?

Psychologist June Tangney and her colleagues have spent decades studying these two emotions, building on earlier work by Helen Block Lewis. In their account, guilt is about a specific behavior, while shame is about the whole self (Tangney et al., 2007). That difference shows up in what each emotion pulls us toward:

  • Guilt tends to lead toward repair: apologizing, making amends, doing better next time
  • Shame tends to lead toward hiding: withdrawing, avoiding, or sometimes lashing out to protect a self that feels exposed

The same research found that a tendency toward shame is linked with depression, anxiety, and low self-esteem, while guilt on its own, untangled from shame, generally isn't. In other words, a healthy capacity for guilt helps us live with each other. Shame, especially the chronic kind, mostly helps us disappear.

Where shame comes from

Shame usually has a history. It often takes root early, in moments when a child's needs, feelings, or ways of being were met with disgust, ridicule, or silence. It can come from families, schools, religious communities, or a culture that tells some people, over and over, that something about them is unacceptable. For many LGBTQ+ people, neurodivergent people, and people living in larger bodies or with chronic illness, shame was handed to them long before they had words to refuse it.

Shame can also hide inside other things: perfectionism, people-pleasing, harsh self-criticism, procrastination, or the feeling of being a fraud no matter how much you accomplish.

How therapy helps with shame

My work with shame draws on two traditions that complement each other.

The first is compassion-focused therapy, or CFT, developed by psychologist Paul Gilbert specifically for people struggling with high levels of shame and self-criticism (Gilbert, 2009). CFT starts from the idea that, for many people, the brain's threat system has been running the show for a long time, while the capacity for soothing and safety has been harder to reach. The work involves building that capacity, not just thinking differently about yourself, but learning to feel a bit safer inside your own mind. A 2017 meta-analysis of 21 randomized trials found that compassion-based interventions led to moderate improvements in self-compassion, depression, anxiety, and well-being, though the authors noted that more research with larger, more diverse samples is needed (Kirby et al., 2017).

The second is psychodynamic therapy, which helps us understand where shame came from and how it still shapes our relationships. Part of what makes this work powerful is the relationship itself. When you say the thing you've never said out loud, and the person across from you stays, something shifts. Being truly seen, and not rejected, is one of the most direct answers to shame there is.

What it can feel like to work on shame

It's worth saying that talking about shame can feel exposing at first, and that's okay. Good therapy goes at your pace. Over time, many people notice:

  • A quieter, less punishing inner voice
  • More freedom to make mistakes without spiraling
  • Less need to hide, perform, or overprepare
  • More capacity for real connection, because there's less to protect

Frequently asked questions

What's the difference between guilt and shame?

Guilt is about a behavior ("I did something bad") and tends to lead toward repair. Shame is about the whole self ("I am bad") and tends to lead toward hiding, withdrawal, or defensiveness.

How does therapy help with shame?

Therapy helps by giving shame a safe place to be spoken and understood. Approaches like compassion-focused therapy build the capacity for self-soothing, and psychodynamic therapy helps you understand where shame came from and how it shapes your relationships.

What is compassion-focused therapy?

Compassion-focused therapy (CFT) was developed by Paul Gilbert for people with high shame and self-criticism. It helps people develop feelings of warmth, safety, and self-compassion to balance an overactive sense of threat.

Is shame linked to depression and anxiety?

Yes. Research has found that a tendency toward shame is associated with depression, anxiety, and low self-esteem, while guilt on its own generally is not.
